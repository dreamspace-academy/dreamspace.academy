The STEAM programmes

Learning by making

How learning by making works in the STEAM programmes, one part of DreamSpace Academy’s education: what stays open, what is fixed, and where a learner starts.

Published 27 August 2026 Updated 29 October 2026

What the core terms mean

The STEAM programmes are one part of DreamSpace Academy’s education; the programmes page lists what else it teaches. All the STEAM programmes share the four ideas below about how learning is organised and assessed. Safety, inclusion, accountability to the community and what is running now are covered on their own pages.

A stage names what the making is for

Explorer, Maker, Innovator and Changemaker describe what the making is for. Stages are named, never numbered, and never graded against one another.

A level measures depth in one course

A level describes how much a learner can do in one subject. It belongs to a course, not to a person: see where depth lives.

Making follows no set sequence

A learner may start from an aim, a material, an idea, a sound, a question or an accident. There is no single order of steps that everyone must follow. A facilitator may teach or show a way of working where it helps, but following it is not what is assessed.

Completion has a fixed standard

Assessed work must show all six kinds of evidence in the Definition of Done, in any order. This is the one thing that does not change.

What can change, and what cannot

The way a learner makes something can change. The standard for finished work cannot. Keeping these two apart lets each subject work in the way that suits it, while all assessed work is held to one standard that can be compared. A fixed cycle that starts with “identify a problem” suits engineering, but it fits music and much of design badly.

FlexibleFixed
What it covers The making process: how a learner gets from nothing to something The completion standard: what must exist before assessed work counts as done
Why A set cycle that opens with “identify a problem” is built for engineering, and does not work for music, art and much of design Without a shared standard, a certificate from one facilitator does not mean the same as one from another
In practice Facilitators plan the order of sessions around the subject, what learners are meant to learn, how ready they are, their access needs and safety Aim, Options, Made, Evaluated, Shared, Refined: all six, in any order

The six are kinds of evidence, not steps. They may appear in any order, repeat or overlap. The standard is never published, taught or drawn as a cycle, because a loop suggests an order that the standard does not ask for.

A facilitator may still walk a group through one possible way of working. What is assessed is that all six are there, not the route taken. The assessment page has the full detail.

Explorer work is not assessed at all. It is open, hands-on exploration led by curiosity, not a set task[5]. Not assessed does not mean left alone. Facilitators keep the work safe, show what the tools can do, answer questions and give feedback when a learner wants it. None of that counts towards a certificate.

The Definition of Done applies exactly where certificates do, from the Maker stage upwards. Assessing exploration would discourage it.

Where a learner starts: showing what they already know

Every course has a recommended route, so a beginner always knows where they are. That route is a suggestion, not a queue.

  • Where a course allows a learner to join partway, the learner shows they have what the course needs (its prerequisites) through a placement check. They do not have to repeat what they have already learned.
  • Earlier learning counts wherever it happened: at school, at work, online, or from a relative who repairs things.
  • Time spent enrolled does not, on its own, move anyone forward. A learner moves on by showing what they can do, finishing work and meeting the prerequisites of what comes next.
  • Moving to the next stage depends on meeting its conditions, not on time served. When someone actually starts still depends on course dates, free places and supervision.

The placement check exists so that age, school year and past enrolment are not used in place of readiness. It asks a learner to show the prerequisites directly. It does not cover everything. Confidence, communication and access needs, recent practice and the support someone will want are all looked at beside the check, not as part of it.

The check matters most where a subject really does build on an earlier idea. Research on how people learn about money shows that understanding profit depends on first being able to compare prices[45], and that saving and selling can be understood far earlier[43].

Those studies report their findings by age because they studied children in school year groups. What they actually found is a prerequisite, so the placement check tests the prerequisite.

Access and inclusion

DreamSpace Academy makes two commitments here.

Age does not determine placement

Age is not used as an entry rule, an age band, a level label or a published “typical age”, because a typical age tends to be read as a rule. Where a learner starts depends on what they can show. Age still matters where the law and safety need it: safeguarding, the legal duty of care and supervision. A course may also have practical requirements for taking part.

Learners hold real decisions

Learners take part more and feel they belong more in making spaces when they make real decisions, and when ways of knowing outside the usual classroom ones are valued[27]. The academy builds that into its courses: real choices inside the work, and more than one way in.

It does not mean the learner must come up with the starting point. A project may begin with a facilitator, a community, a partner or a brief, as long as the learner has real room to interpret it and shape what happens next.

Access to equipment matters too. In Sri Lanka, national statistics record that 19.1% of households own a computer, with the Eastern Province at 10.7% against 31.4% in the Western Province[58].

A class that expects learners to have a computer at home ends up serving the learners who need it least. International guidance makes the same point: technology in education has to be judged by whose terms it is brought in on[52].

Where the approach comes from

DreamSpace Academy built the approach from well-known ideas in education. The table shows which idea comes from where.

Here, PBL means project-based learning. Problem-based learning is a separate, well-established method with a different history and structure[16]. The STEAM programmes do not build on it.

The main argument against this approach

Any way of teaching that relies on discovery and open-ended projects has to answer a serious, well-argued objection. It says that teaching with very little guidance does not work, especially for beginners. Learning by solving problems only works once learners already know enough to solve them[17].

That objection is largely correct. The STEAM programmes answer it in four ways.

  • Learners who know least get the most guidance. Explorer work is hands-on exploration with a facilitator, not discovery left to the learner alone. In the Maker stage, taught content and clear briefs build skills early. As those skills grow, the learner takes more charge of deciding and directing the work. To move on from the Maker stage, a learner needs a project they chose and carried through themselves.
  • Learners get more freedom as they need less support, never the other way round. Research on maker and technology education describes the same pattern: a first workshop is narrowly defined, and later ones leave more ways to solve the problem with less support[15].
  • Structured teaching is a full part of how the programmes are taught. See facilitation.
  • Every stage is open-ended. What changes is what is at stake and how much support there is. Explorer work is open and low-stakes. Maker work pairs taught skills with more and more choice. Innovator and Changemaker work take those skills into problems and commitments beyond the exercise. In the later stages, what changes is the consequence, not the openness.

The disagreement in the research is mostly about when to guide, more than about “discovery versus instruction”. DreamSpace Academy’s answer is that the amount of guidance should depend on what the learner already knows. That is also why the placement check tests what they know directly.
