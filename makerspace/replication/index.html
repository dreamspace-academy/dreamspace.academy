Spreading the MakerSpace

Replication

How one working makerspace becomes many as a social franchise: the Starter Unit, the licence to run the academy's programmes, what is held centrally, what is local, and the risks that close community makerspaces.

Published 27 August 2026 Updated 29 October 2026

Why a social franchise

A single makerspace in one town helps one town. So DreamSpace Academy designed the MakerSpace so that local partners in rural and underserved communities can set up their own, all held to the same quality standards. This is done as a social franchise.

In a social franchise, the reason for spreading is the mission, not profit. Local partners set up Starter Units in spaces they provide in their own communities. Each unit follows the academy’s design, operating standard and branding. Any success is meant to go back into the work, not be taken out as profit.

What is franchised is the MakerSpace itself: the design of the unit, the brand and the operating standard. DreamSpace Academy’s programmes are not part of it. A franchisee takes a licence to run them in the unit. The same programmes also run in schools, at partner sites and online. The academy’s labs and ventures are not part of the franchise at all.

There are many ways to spread work that succeeds. At one end, an organisation simply publishes what it knows. At the other, it owns and runs every site itself. Between them sit looser arrangements such as affiliation[48]. Each step along that range trades control against reach.

Social franchising sits in the middle. The established model for it names four things to get right: ownership, process, an enhanced network, and the name[49].

A finding from research on scaling: sharing decisions with local sites works better than copying one site exactly under central control. That is how one values-driven enterprise grew from one hub to more than a hundred[51].

Starter Unit: the smallest working site

The unit that spreads is small. A Starter Unit is one site, equipped to run the core activities well rather than to impress, at the lowest cost that still meets the standard.

What is in it

  • Electronics and soldering station
  • 3D printing
  • A small CNC machine with a laser module
  • Robotics kits
  • Workbench and furniture
  • The means to run digital-education and AI programmes
  • The means to make products for sale

A complete set-up

The Starter Unit is the full working set-up. It is not a smaller copy of a bigger site, waiting to grow. Research on makerspaces in places with few resources agrees that tools that can be maintained locally, often simple ones, are the right choice. Equipment that is more advanced than needed ends up unused and becomes a burden[28, 26].

The cheapest technique in the whole curriculum, cardboard prototyping (making early models out of cardboard), can be started anywhere without any money to set it up. It is also one of the techniques that lasts longest.

What is fixed and what is local

The academy sets the structure: the levels, their order, the learning objectives, and what a certificate means. This is the same in every unit. That is what lets a certificate earned in one unit mean the same thing as one earned three hundred kilometres away.

Each unit’s trainer writes the challenges and projects used inside each level. The trainer updates them for every batch (each new group of learners) to fit the community the unit is actually in. So every unit runs the same courses, applied to its own community’s problems.

The cost of writing local projects

Because the trainer writes the projects, this work has to be done in every unit, again and again. Trainers already report it as their hardest recurring task, harder than the teaching itself.

If nothing is done about it, it becomes the franchise’s main ongoing cost and its main risk to quality. The structure would stay the same everywhere, but what learners actually experience would depend on whoever happened to be writing projects that month.

Shared project bank: challenges every unit draws on

The shared project bank is the answer to that cost. It is a common stock of challenges and projects that every unit draws from and contributes back to. It gives trainers projects to start from, without taking away their local choice.

  • Draw and contribute. Units add projects as well as use them. A bank that only handed projects out would be central writing under another name, and would take back control of exactly the part that is meant to be local.
  • A trainer who did not write an entry must be able to run it. So each entry gives the problem, the level it fits, the materials, the build steps, and what “done” looks like. Anything less is a note, not an entry.
  • A stock to draw from. It must never become a fixed list of projects. It saves trainers from starting with a blank page, but the choice stays theirs.
  • The structure does not change. The levels, their order, the objectives and what a certificate means are still set by the academy.

Two questions about the bank are still open: whether contributing is required or voluntary, and whether project quality is checked centrally or left to each unit.

Operators commit to:

  • the guidelines and standards;
  • approved branding;
  • quality and safety, in teaching and in machine use;
  • the assessment and certification process;
  • reporting regularly;
  • working in line with the mission.

The terms of the licence, and what an operator contributes in return, have not been settled yet.

How a Starter Unit pays its way

The plan for a Starter Unit has two sources of income: training fees and the sale of products made in the unit. At starter size it keeps margins (the money left after costs) modest: one trainer, and a cap on the number of batches each month, rather than a bigger margin bought by overloading the trainer.

The plan assumes a unit settles down in three to six months and has steady income by six to twelve months. These would improve the picture:

  • subsidised or shared space;
  • orders from schools, small businesses and events;
  • higher-value products;
  • premium bootcamps;
  • more staff in a second phase.
About this plan

This is a plan, not a track record. It rests on the academy’s own assumptions about enrolment, orders and rent.

A published fee is what is charged, not what is collected. The academy's own courses have run with far fewer learners than planned, so a price multiplied by a planned enrolment gives a number that has never happened.

Two widely repeated statistics about how social enterprises survive and scale did not hold up when checked, so they are not used here.

How far the model has got

A widely used sequence for scaling has five phases: prove, design, systemise, pilot and scale[48].

PhaseFocusStatus
ProveDocument what already works, from the existing track recordMostly done
Design & systemiseWrite down the model, curriculum and quality standards, and set a starting point for measuring resultsIn progress, this site is part of it
PilotThe first Starter Units set up by partners; collect real figures on costs, income and resultsNot yet
ScaleA network of many units, where each new unit makes the others strongerNot yet

The model is at an early stage. The academy is asking for funding because the move from design to pilot is a known point where efforts to spread social programmes run short of patient capital: long-term money that does not expect a quick return[50].

Expected risks and how the model answers them

Community makerspaces close for known reasons: unstable finances, weak community involvement, too few resources, and weak local leadership. They last when the community owns them and the money comes from several sources rather than a single grant[31, 26]. The academy built the list of risks below from those findings.

RiskWhat answers it
Unstable finances, a unit that does not pay its waySeveral sources of funding, a modest starter set-up, and the ways to raise income listed above
Weak community involvementThe community owns the challenges, and open making time is protected
Weak local leadershipChoosing the trainer together, a planned induction, and ongoing support
Tools that cannot be maintained locallyEquipment chosen from the start to be maintained locally, often simple
Trainers worn out by writing projectsThe shared project bank
“Earn” producing less than hopedMeasuring against a starting point, and designing for lasting income rather than assuming it
Learners leaving for a job market that does not match their skillsContinued support through the network, and ways to stay connected, rather than a hard end to the programme
