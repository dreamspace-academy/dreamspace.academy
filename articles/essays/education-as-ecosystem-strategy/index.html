Why an Ecosystem Builder Has to Teach

Why DreamSpace Academy runs classes itself: teaching is where the people who build and keep up an innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem come from.

Updated 12 October 2026

This is an argument, not a policy page

It makes a case from DreamSpace Academy’s own record, sets out the argument against it, and marks how well each claim is backed by evidence. For what the academy actually runs, the main pages are the description to rely on. This essay gives the reasoning behind them.

Education is how the ecosystem gets built

One of three essays on how the academy’s education and the rest of the organisation fit together.

In short. DreamSpace Academy describes itself as an Innovation & Entrepreneurship Ecosystem Builder: it helps a place grow the people, links and support that new ideas and businesses need. An organisation like that has to run education itself rather than buy it in. Section 5 sets out the five ways DreamSpace Academy does this.

Evidence marks. Accounts of what happened come from DreamSpace Academy’s own reports. What the academy has decided comes from its written plans. Each claim carries a mark: 🟢 backed by original documents or by more than one source; 🟡 supported, but only in part; 🔴 stated, and still being tested.

1. Why an ecosystem builder runs education

People expect an ecosystem builder to bring people together, make introductions, run an incubator, find money for new businesses and put on a demo day. Running a robotics class for eleven-year-olds in a rural government school is not on that list. It looks like another organisation’s work.

DreamSpace Academy sees it the other way round. In an underserved community, education is the only part of the job that produces what everything else needs: people who will build. Every other part of the job assumes there are already founders, makers, trainers and partners to work with. Where there are not, someone has to bring them through, and teaching is the only way to do that.

Two facts pull in opposite directions here. First, the shortage of such people is what holds the ecosystem back. Second, education on its own does not produce income. This is well evidenced, and DreamSpace Academy’s own written analysis says so. The academy accepts both.

2. What ecosystem theory says, and its limits

Researchers who study entrepreneurial ecosystems are open about the idea’s weak points. Its leading writers call the concept “under-developed and under-theorized”. The most common complaint is that the idea goes round in a circle: an ecosystem is whatever produces successful businesses, and wherever there are successful businesses there must be a good ecosystem [Stam 2015; Stam & van de Ven 2021]. A review of 181 studies found that many use the term “in name only” [Wurth et al. 2023].

The review of entrepreneurial ecosystem sets out the full definition and its limits.

Two points from that research hold up despite the criticism, and both explain why an ecosystem builder should teach.

The parts only matter when they work together. Isenberg, who first set out the idea in 2010, says that leadership, culture, capital markets and customers are “in isolation… conducive to entrepreneurship but insufficient to sustain it. That’s where many governmental efforts go wrong: they address only one or two elements.” So a list of the universities, mentors, investors and events in a place does not show that an ecosystem exists.

Entrepreneurs build the system as well as coming out of it [Stam 2015]. This puts education at the centre. If people build the system, then having enough of them is something needed at the start, not a result counted at the end. Where the ecosystem is missing, those people are missing too.

The research also carries a warning that governments often ignore: stop copying Silicon Valley. Reviews find that governments commonly copy a model from a successful ecosystem elsewhere without asking whether it fits local conditions [Spigel 2017; Brown & Mawson 2019]. An accelerator in a district with no founders has nobody to accelerate. The problem there is a shortage of people, and education is how that shortage is filled.

3. The constraint DreamSpace Academy found: identifying changemakers

DreamSpace Academy’s hardest constraint was never the labs, the funding or the curriculum. It was identifying changemakers.

Reaching people was not the hard part. Anyone could apply, and many did. Field visits, open calls for applications and DreamSpace Academy’s own bootcamps brought in large numbers, and most of the changemakers so far arrived that way. One open call in October 2022 ran across four districts and drew 360 applications, of which 30 were selected through district workshops and one-to-one interviews 🟢.

The hard part was that the old approach waited for people who were already motivated to come forward. Such people are rare, and finding them cannot be planned or grown. The supply is unpredictable and tiring to keep up: every search has to be run again the next year, from zero.

So DreamSpace Academy changed its approach:

Co-creation changes the supply from found to built. The Changemaker Journey (Explorer, Maker, Innovator, Changemaker) develops the motivation rather than screening for people who arrive with it. Finding changemakers stops being a search and becomes something the academy teaches.

A search has no schedule, and nobody knows how many people it will find. Teaching has a curriculum, a timetable, a cohort (a group that starts and finishes together) and a rate that can be measured. Education is what lets an ecosystem builder make that change. Nothing else on the usual ecosystem-builder list does.

4. The main argument against: education does not produce income

The strongest objection to this argument is “education does not produce livelihoods, and the data says so.”

The data does say so. DreamSpace Academy’s own analysis of why young people in these regions struggle to earn rests on these national statistics:

  • Unemployment rises with education in Sri Lanka: 0.7% at Grade 5 and below, 3.4% at Grades 6 to 10, 6.0% at O/L, 8.0% at A/L and above, and the gradient holds in every province 🟢.
  • 67.6% of Sri Lanka’s unemployed are already computer literate, 75.5% of those aged 20 to 24, 81.2% of those aged 25 to 29 🟢.

Two out of three unemployed Sri Lankans can already use a computer. Computer literacy alone does not guarantee work; market access and other support matter as well.

Studies of training programmes reach the same answer from the other side. Training on its own does little or nothing for income, and what it does often fades:

  • only about 29% of short-term earnings estimates are positive;
  • a review of 37 evaluations finds “no immediate translation into business set-up or increased income”;
  • the most careful randomised trials of skills training show the effects fading by 12 to 15 months 🟢.

Training plus something else is a different matter. A job referral raised earnings by 40% where training alone did nothing. Programmes that combined several kinds of help produced gains that lasted, across six countries. In post-conflict Uganda, cash plus training plus supervision doubled incomes 🟢. Peer-reviewed research on FabLabs (shared workshops with digital making tools) finds that making builds some entrepreneurial skills, and needs dedicated programmes and business support on top 🟢.

The academy calls these extra inputs couplings: the money, access to buyers, introductions and continued support that turn a skill into a livelihood. Its analysis concludes that what breaks the link between skill and income is the lack of these couplings, not a lack of training.

DreamSpace Academy’s position is therefore narrower than “education works”:

Education is the mechanism that produces builders. It is not the mechanism that produces income. An organisation that teaches without the couplings will watch the gains decay on a schedule the literature can already predict.

This is why the teaching sits inside an ecosystem builder rather than inside a school. A school can offer only the first half. DreamSpace Academy’s education depends on what comes with it: the labs, the bridging into partners’ work, the seed money, the mentors and contact that lasts for years.

Several of these exist today:

  • an impact-loan fund and co-founding of ventures, both held by DreamSpace (Pvt) Ltd rather than the Academy;
  • bridging and access to buyers;
  • contact that lasts for years.

But they have never been put together as one planned package, or measured as one.

5. How DreamSpace Academy uses education to build the ecosystem

The teaching builds the ecosystem in five separate ways. Lumped together as “programmes”, they look like a training centre. Taken one at a time, each one builds a different part of the ecosystem.

① Empowerment Inside School: the school programme

Empowerment Inside School (EIS) is DreamSpace Academy’s main programme inside schools. It teaches ICT that follows the Sri Lankan government syllabus, plus Robotics & STEAM, at no charge to underserved schools. It is planned to stay with the same pupils for six years, from Grade 6 to Grade 11. Pupils learn by working on challenges rather than by memorising.

The programme follows a written procedure with 13 phases 🟢. The main steps are:

  • choosing schools that have no ICT teacher, no computer lab, low O/L ICT results, rural or marginalised;
  • getting Zonal Education Department approval;
  • testing pupils at the start, so there is a baseline;
  • teaching;
  • testing again at the end and comparing with the start;
  • closing, which trains teachers and leaves a student ICT club behind.

The programme’s own reports give these figures:

  • Batticaloa pilot, July to December 2025, 5 schools: “Reached 194 students across Grades 6–9”;
  • Hatton, across Dickoya and Ireby, late 2025: 306 students;
  • four Nuwara Eliya schools, April to May 2026: 114 students, 13 sessions, 21 projects 🟢.

② The Changemaker Journey: where it runs, including the MakerSpace

The STEAM programmes are one part of what DreamSpace Academy teaches. The academy also runs business and entrepreneurship training, digital security training, and workplace and teacher training.

The Changemaker Journey is the path learners follow in the STEAM programmes. It can run in schools, at partner sites, online and in the academy’s own spaces.

MakerSpace by DreamSpace Academy is something narrower. It is a design for a physical space (the equipment list and layout) plus a standard for running it. It is built to be copied and franchised in a space that a local partner provides, and it is one of the places the journey can run. It is not the organisation itself, and the academy’s education does not depend on it.

So far the academy has run several curriculum tracks and its own bootcamps 🟢:

  • seven numbered robotics batches, two of which split into two groups running side by side;
  • a music programme in three batches, with graded exams in two languages;
  • AI & Electronics with Scratch;
  • Make & Play;
  • a programme preparing students for university;
  • monthly one-day workshops with prepared agendas and slides.

The essay How DreamSpace Teaches covers the teaching method.

DreamSpace Academy has no standing labs. Software, media and music work is done by groups of people who share a craft and learn from each other, not by standing units. An older published list of eleven labs describes the past, not the present.

④ Bridging: placing local people in partners’ programmes

Bridging is not teaching, but it is something the academy offers that outside programmes do not. The academy knows the region and its people, which an international programme cannot buy, and bridging turns that local network into work for local people.

The clearest example is in the director’s own words: “IREX wanted to spread its media-literacy programme across Sri Lanka and they didn’t have a good partner, DreamSpace Academy brought them to the Central and the North… I introduced Ladhushan and Donald as trainers for their training programme”, and they trained about 60 people 🟢.

Bridging takes four forms:

  • placement: putting individuals into a funder’s programme as paid trainers;
  • devolution: handing school clusters to local bodies (③ above);
  • convening: a partner’s premises and reputation host a DreamSpace Academy event;
  • exposure: DreamSpace Academy uses its international standing to put its own people on a stage.

Bridging is not a revenue model. It is a way of getting work done. How it is paid for (trainer day rates, the terms for EIS clusters, and anything the academy earns as the go-between) is not published here.

⑤ Alumni: returning as teachers and directors

Alumni now lead much of the software, media and music work, and two are directors 🟢. When DreamSpace Academy took its approach to Erode, Tamil Nadu, it was “delivered by two people who are themselves products of the pathway”. A large share of the people who teach for DreamSpace Academy came through DreamSpace Academy.

The limit: these are Batticaloa-trained people travelling to a new place. That is not yet evidence that a new site can grow its own teachers. The one test of that failed. DreamSpace Mullaitivu closed because nobody took over from the person running it: that person could not continue, no successor with the interest was found, and the search looked only locally. Funding, day-to-day running and skills were not the cause. Any claim that the approach can be copied to new places has to count the site that closed as well.

6. Sri Lanka’s school reform and the gap in rural schools

Sri Lanka’s curriculum reform makes ICT and “Entrepreneurship & Financial Literacy” compulsory essential subjects in Grades 6 to 9 for the first time. It phases in from 2026 and covers all four grades by 2029 🟢.

The means to teach them were never built:

  • 19% of type 2 and type 3 schools have IT labs, against 72% of top-tier schools 🟢. (Type 2 and 3 are the smaller government schools that do not teach up to A/L.)
  • In the technology stream there are 35.3 students per teacher, against an average of 12.8 🟢.
  • At home, 1.5% of estate households own a computer, and 92.6% of estate internet access is by smartphone alone.

A phone is not enough to learn CAD, coding or a laser cutter.

The government’s STEM reform shows there is demand for this work. The state has promised something it cannot yet deliver in rural schools. That gives the academy a place as a partner that supports the state system and feeds into it, not as a rival.

Whether that place can be made formal (an MoU, a provincial agreement, recognition as a teaching partner) is still an open question. The need is proven. DreamSpace Academy’s formal right to act on it is not. What exists today is narrower: written Zonal Education Office approvals in three zones (Batticaloa, Kantale, Nuwara Eliya).

National policy makes a related point in its own words: Sri Lanka’s startup ecosystem is “confined to the urban areas especially Colombo”, and “decentralizing support infrastructure” is named as a national policy priority. DreamSpace Academy’s work carries out that priority.

7. BOOKBRIDGE: an education programme that produced an organisation

DreamSpace Academy began in BOOKBRIDGE’s 11th Capability Program in 2018. The programme had four modules, ran for about six months and ended with a pitch to investors. As BOOKBRIDGE publishes it, each centre is set up as a social enterprise run by a local entrepreneur, paid for by a soft loan (a loan on easy terms) that is repaid from the centre’s own trading income 🟢.

So an education programme produced an organisation: DreamSpace Academy itself.

The details of the loan, including whose it was and whether it was repaid, have not been confirmed. This story is not an argument against grants.

DreamSpace Academy is funded from three sources: grants and partnerships, revenue it earns itself, and the founders’ own capital. The academy is working out how much each one contributes, and will say so here once that is known. It is not trying to become independent of grants. It is building work that keeps running after the grant that paid for it stops.

8. What DreamSpace Academy does not claim

The argument above explains why an ecosystem builder should teach. It does not claim that DreamSpace Academy’s ecosystem has yet produced what it aims at.

  • It is not yet known how many learners become Changemakers. The academy is setting up records of how many people start the pathway. Until that count exists, there is nothing to compare the Changemakers against, and nothing can be said about how well the pathway works as a whole, only that each part of it is backed by evidence. 🔴
  • “Earn” is a hypothesis being tested, not a result. That a skilled maker can sell things they have made for a small income is near-term and low-risk 🟡. That this becomes a lasting livelihood at a reliable rate, in this place, through this pathway, has not been measured 🔴. No figure for jobs created exists, and none is published.
  • What the ecosystem has produced is mostly unmeasured. The description Innovation & Entrepreneurship Ecosystem Builder rests on what DreamSpace Academy has built, not yet on what the ecosystem has produced.
  • Reported outcomes so far come from people who finished. Following up learners who leave is being worked on, and what it finds will be published.

9. Summary of the argument

Entrepreneurs build an innovation and entrepreneurship ecosystem as well as filling it. In a community where those people are not already there, an ecosystem builder has two options: wait for them to appear, or bring them through.

Waiting is a search with no schedule and no promised result. DreamSpace Academy did that for years and named it as its hardest problem. Bringing people through is teaching, with a curriculum, a cohort and a rate that can be measured.

The evidence also shows that teaching alone does not produce income. So the teaching has to be joined to labs, bridges into partners’ work, money and contact that lasts for years, not just to a certificate. Those links are the ecosystem, and education brings it the people.

A note on sources

This essay draws on DreamSpace Academy’s own reports, case studies, accounts from its director and the history of its funders, together with its written plans: its description of who it is, its analysis of why young people struggle to earn, its theory of change, the DreamSpace Lifecycle and the incubation model.

National statistics come from their publishers: the Department of Census and Statistics Labour Force Survey 2023 and Computer Literacy Bulletin 2024, UNDP and OPHI district profiles 2023, the World Bank Sri Lanka Development Update (October 2024), and the WIPO Global Innovation Index 2025. Intervention evidence is cited to the published studies named in the text; the full list is on the references page.

Some figures often quoted on this subject have been shown to be wrong, among them the “90% franchise versus 30% startup survival” statistic and a branded “Five Stages of Scale”. Neither is used here.

A third party repeating DreamSpace Academy’s own words is not independent evidence. Partner pages, directories, award listings and news items that quote the academy are repeating it, not reporting something they saw, and none of them is treated here as confirming anything.
